Lesson 0.04
★ El imperfecto — la cámara que sigue rodando
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The easiest tense in Spanish to build, and the hardest to know when to use.
Yesterday's preterite reports what happened. The imperfecto does something different: it describes what was going on — the scene, the habit, the weather, the way things used to be. It has exactly three irregular verbs in the entire language.

So the forms will take you ten minutes. The rest of the lesson is about its five jobs, because knowing when to reach for it is what tomorrow's contrast lesson depends on.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to conjugate any verb in the imperfect, including ir, ser and ver.
2
I will be able to name the five jobs of the imperfect and recognise each one in a sentence.
3
I will be able to describe how things used to be when I was a child, in connected sentences.
⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 5 min

A camera, not a photograph

Hold this image for the next two lessons, because it does most of the work: the pretérito is a photograph — one click, one finished moment. The imperfecto is a camera left running — no beginning, no end in view, just what was going on. Llovió = it rained (and stopped). Llovía = it was raining (and we are inside the scene).

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
In English, which of these feels closest to the imperfect to you?
🧠 PART 2 · LAS FORMAS 10 min

Two endings and three exceptions. That is all.

-ar verbs take -aba; -er and -ir verbs share -ía. Nothing else changes — no stem changes, no spelling tricks, no strong forms.

Subject-ar · hablar-er · comer-ir · vivir

Two things to notice. Every -er/-ir form carries an accent on the í — comía, comías, comíamos — without exception. And in -ar verbs only the nosotros form is accented: hablábamos. Also note yo and él/ella are identical, so the imperfect often needs its subject pronoun stated where the preterite does not.

⭐ PARA IR MÁS LEJOS · optional · +4 min
Three irregular verbs, in a tense used constantly, in a language with thousands of verbs. Compare that to the preterite's eight strong stems plus three spelling families. If you have ever suspected that Spanish rewards the patient, the imperfect is your evidence.
🧠 PART 3 · LOS CINCO TRABAJOS 11 min

Five jobs, and what each one sounds like in English

JobSpanishEnglish clue
1. Habit — repeated action, no endpointDe niño jugaba al fútbol todos los sábados.used to / would
2. Scene in progressMi madre cocinaba mientras yo estudiaba.was …-ing
3. Description — people, places, weather, feelingsLa casa era pequeña y hacía mucho calor.was / were
4. Age and timeTenía ocho años. Eran las tres de la tarde.was / it was
5. Mental & emotional statesQuería ir, pero no sabía la dirección.wanted / knew

Job 4 is worth memorising as a rule with no exceptions: age and clock time in the past are always imperfect. «Tuve ocho años» is not a thing. And job 5 explains why quería, sabía, podía, creía appear in the imperfect far more often than in the preterite — states linger, and lingering is what this tense is for.

✅ TRY IT · name the job
«Eran las once de la noche y llovía sin parar.» Which two jobs is the imperfect doing?

Ahora tú: describe your life at age eight in four sentences — use at least three different jobs from Part 3. Say it aloud first.

Cuando tenía ocho años, vivía en una casa pequeña cerca del parque. Todos los días caminaba a la escuela con mi hermana. Mi maestra era muy paciente y siempre nos leía cuentos por la tarde. Yo quería ser veterinario porque me gustaban mucho los animales.

Jobs used: age (tenía), description (era, vivía), habit (caminaba, leía), mental state (quería, gustaban). Not a single preterite in it — and it does not need one, because nothing in it is an event.
📖 PART 5 · A LEER 5 min

Los veranos de antes

Cuando éramos pequeños, mis primos y yo pasábamos todo el verano en casa de mi abuela. La casa era vieja y tenía un patio enorme con dos árboles de mango. Por la mañana hacía tanto calor que nadie quería salir; nos quedábamos dentro y jugábamos a las cartas. Mi abuela cocinaba y escuchaba la radio, y a veces cantaba canciones que nosotros no conocíamos. Íbamos a la playa solamente cuando bajaba el sol. Yo creía que esos veranos no iban a terminar nunca.

⭐ COMPRUEBA · optional
Every bolded verb is imperfect, and there is not one preterite in the entire paragraph. Notice what that does: the text has no plot. Nothing happens — and that is precisely the point of the tense. Tomorrow you will put one preterite into a paragraph like this and watch it suddenly acquire a story.
🌎 CULTURA · EL MUNDO HISPANO
Los abuelos en casa

The reading above is a common shape of childhood memory across the Spanish-speaking world: summers at a grandparent's house, often with cousins, often for weeks rather than days. Multigenerational households remain far more usual in Latin America and Spain than in the United States — grandparents living with or beside the family rather than at a distance.

That is worth knowing as more than a fact. It means that when you narrate your own childhood in Spanish to a Spanish speaker, the frame they expect — who was around, whose house — may differ from yours. Narrating well across cultures involves noticing which details your listener will find ordinary and which will surprise them.

★ LA VIRTUD · LA DILIGENCIA

The imperfect is where diligence pays visibly, because the tense is so easy to form that it is tempting to skip the accents. But vivia and vivía are not the same word to a reader, and the ten seconds you spend adding the accent today are ten seconds you will not spend losing a point in eight weeks. Lo fácil también merece cuidado.

★ Las direcciones — la forma de una indicación

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Good directions are not more detailed. They are better ordered.
Yesterday you learned the parts of a city. Today you add the verbs of movement — seguir derecho, doblar, cruzar — and something more useful than vocabulary: the four-part shape a set of directions takes in any language.

Still no new grammar. Today you use infinitives and the «tiene que» frame; tomorrow you get the command forms that make it sound native.

⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 6 min

Why most directions fail

Think about the last time someone gave you directions you could not follow. It was almost certainly not a vocabulary problem — it was that they gave you six steps when you could hold three, or never told you what you were looking for at the end.

Good directions have a shape: how far, which turns, what you will see, and a fallback. Part 3 makes that explicit.

⭐ PARA IR MÁS LEJOS · optional · +5 min
Doblar and girar both mean to turn and are largely interchangeable for directions; doblar is more common in Latin America, and it also means to fold. Both take a: doblar a la derecha.

Seguir derecho is worth learning as a unit. Careful with the near-neighbour la derecha (the right): derecho = straight ahead, a la derecha = to the right. One letter, opposite instructions — and a genuinely common way to get lost.
🧠 PART 3 · LOS CUATRO MOVIMIENTOS 9 min

Four moves, in this order

MoveWhat it doesExample (with tiene que)
1. La distanciaCommit them for a stretchTiene que seguir derecho tres cuadras.
2. El giroOne turn, with a markerTiene que doblar a la izquierda en el semáforo.
3. La referenciaWhat they will see — the reassuranceEl museo queda enfrente de la plaza.
4. El plan BWhat to do if it goes wrongSi se pierde, puede preguntar en la tienda.

Move 3 is the one people leave out, and it is the most valuable. A landmark tells the listener whether they are still right — without it they have to trust their counting for three blocks with no way to check.

💡 POR AHORA: TIENE QUE + INFINITIVO
You do not have the command forms yet, so today use tiene que + infinitive — tiene que seguir, tiene que doblar. It is correct, polite, natural Spanish and it uses only the present tense you have had since Spanish 1.

Tomorrow you learn the shorter, more idiomatic version: siga, doble. Both are good Spanish. Tiene que is slightly softer, which is occasionally exactly what you want.
🧠 PART 4 · PRACTÍCALO 8 min
✅ TRY IT · one letter, opposite instructions
You want to tell someone to keep going straight ahead. Which is right?
✅ TRY IT · which move is missing?

✅ TRY IT · the plan B
«Si ___ , puede preguntar en cualquier tienda.» (perderse, usted)

Ahora tú: give directions from your front door to the nearest shop, using all four moves and tiene que + infinitive.

Tiene que salir y seguir derecho dos cuadras hasta el semáforo. Ahí tiene que doblar a la derecha. La tienda queda al lado de la parada del autobús, enfrente de un parque pequeño. Si se pierde, puede llamarme.

Check yours: all four moves present · derecho for straight and a la derecha for right · and a landmark in move 3, not just a count of blocks.
📖 PART 5 · A LEER 5 min

Perdida en Cartagena

La primera tarde en Cartagena me perdí completamente. El centro histórico es un laberinto de calles estrechas y todas las esquinas se parecen: casas de colores, balcones con flores, la misma luz.

Le pregunté a una señora que vendía fruta. No me dio ni una sola distancia. Me dijo: «Usted tiene que caminar hasta la iglesia amarilla, la que se ve desde aquí. Ahí tiene que doblar a la izquierda. Al ver el mar, ya llegó.»

Y funcionó. No conté cuadras ni miré el mapa: caminé hacia una iglesia amarilla y después hacia el mar. En una ciudad donde las calles no tienen números, las referencias valen más que las distancias.

⭐ COMPRUEBA · optional
The fruit seller gave two of the four moves — the turn and the reference points — and skipped distance entirely. In Cartagena's old town that is the right call, and it is the opposite of the Bogotá grid you read about yesterday. Two Colombian cities, two systems.

Notice the phrase «al ver el mar» — al + infinitive, meaning “upon seeing”. It is a compact construction worth stealing: al salir, al llegar, al cruzar. Spanish uses it constantly where English needs a whole clause. Reading closely for a construction like that is normal; it is the habit Módulo 0 asked you to build.
🌎 CULTURA · COLOMBIA
Bogotá se calcula, Cartagena se camina

Two Colombian cities, two entirely different relationships with an address. Bogotá is a numbered grid at 2,600 metres in the Andes, where a direction is arithmetic. Cartagena, on the Caribbean coast, has a walled colonial centre — a UNESCO World Heritage site — where the streets are narrow, curved, and named rather than numbered, and where people navigate by churches, plazas and colours.

That difference is worth knowing because it changes what a helpful answer looks like. In Bogotá, «está a ocho cuadras» is genuinely useful. In Cartagena it is nearly meaningless, and «hasta la iglesia amarilla» is what gets you there.

Colombians switch between the two without thinking about it. Noticing which mode a place runs on is a real travel skill.

★ LA VIRTUD · LA CORTESÍA

The fruit seller did something courteous that costs nothing and is easy to miss: she gave directions the listener could use, not the ones that were most accurate. She could have counted streets. Instead she picked a yellow church visible from where they stood. La cortesía in giving help is largely a matter of thinking from the other person's position. Dar bien una indicación es imaginar el camino desde los ojos del otro.

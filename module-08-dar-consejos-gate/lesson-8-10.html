Last lesson of the semester. One quiz stands between here and the other side of it.
Work the review, then take the Grammar Quiz (20 pts, two attempts, includes listening). This one is different from the seven before it: it is cumulative, it is the semester gate, and it needs 16 out of 20.

Then read Part 4 properly rather than skimming it. It is an account of what you can do in Spanish today that you could not do in August, and it is worth more of your attention than the score.

🔒 ESTA ES LA PUERTA · read both halves
This quiz is the gate for Semester 1, and it works in two ways at once, which is worth understanding rather than discovering.

1. It needs 16 out of 20 — 80%. Not to pass the module; to open the next one.

2. The next module stays locked until you get there. Not as a threat: as arithmetic. Everything after this point in the course assumes you can decide between an infinitive and a que clause without thinking about it, because that decision sits underneath every structure Semester 2 adds on top. A student who is 60% sure of it does not have a small gap — they have a gap that compounds weekly.

You have two attempts and the higher one counts, and there is nothing on it that has not been taught. This is a fortnight of work you have already done, arranged in twenty questions.
⭐ CÓMO USAR DOS INTENTOS
Take attempt 1 seriously and then stop. Its value is telling you which of the ten rows in Part 1 is weak — reopen that lesson, work it, and come back. Students who take both attempts back to back almost always score about the same twice, having learned nothing in between, and on a gated quiz that is an expensive way to spend your second attempt.

🎉 PART 4 · LO QUE PUEDES HACER AHORA 9 min

Ninety lessons ago it was August

Semesters end with a number and a grade, and neither of those tells you anything about what happened to you. So here it is concretely — not “you have learned a lot”, but the specific things you can do in Spanish today and could not do on the first day of Módulo 0.

You can now…Which you could not do before
Tell the story of something that happened to you — and choose, sentence by sentence, whether the verb reports an event or a backgroundMódulo 0. In August you knew both past tenses and guessed between them.
Say what has changed and what had already happened before that — he aprendido, ya había decidido — with hecho, dicho, visto, escrito, puesto correctMódulos 1–2. Two whole tenses that did not exist for you in September.
Give a stranger directions in a city you have never visited, in Ud. commands, with pronouns attached and the tildes in the right placesMódulo 3.
Tell a friend what not to do without sounding cold — no lo dejes, dímeloMódulo 4.
Explain a purpose and a cause without guessing — por and para, decided by function rather than by a memorised listMódulo 5, and this is the one most Spanish students never stop guessing at.
Describe something and qualify the description in the same sentence — que, quien, lo queMódulo 6. It is what makes writing sound adult rather than assembled.
Ask somebody for something without ordering them — in a mood you had not heard of in September and had, in fact, been conjugating since OctoberMódulos 7–8.
Hold that for a whole paragraph, choosing between four registers as you go, and give the reasonsMódulo 8. This is the Honors difference, and it is the thing employers and universities actually notice.

And one more, which is easy to undersell. You have read eight authentic Spanish texts this semester, unadapted. José Martí on a childhood in Cuba. A real travel guide to Bogotá, written by a volunteer for travellers. A Spanish encyclopedia article on the Galápagos, and another on Las meninas. A Peruvian tradición from 1896 with a pedlar begging for a loan. And, yesterday, a Chilean village schoolteacher's prayer from 1922, in 1922 Spanish, in which you identified the mood of every verb in the opening sentence.

Nobody simplified any of those for you. That is what the Lectura interpretativa strand was for, and it is why this course has five graded components instead of four.

🌎 CULTURA · CHILE
Lo que te llevas de Chile

Six things from this module worth keeping. The shape: 4,300 kilometres long, under 180 wide, so that the desert and the ice belong to the same country and nothing about travelling there is intuitive. The sky: the Atacama's dry air holds ALMA and the Very Large Telescope, and Chilean astronomers hold a guaranteed 10% of the observing time. Two Nobel Prizes in literature from twenty million people — Mistral in 1945, Neruda in 1971. And ¿cachai?, plus al tiro and po, in the fastest Spanish in the Americas.

Two more that belong together, because they are the same story told twice. Valparaíso is a port built up the sides of forty-odd hills, and to get people up them the city built ascensores — funicular railways, some from the 1880s, running on rails at absurd angles. Of the thirty or so built, fewer than half survive and only a handful run on any given day, but the historic quarter has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2003 largely because of them.

And 27 February 2010: a magnitude 8.8 earthquake off the Maule coast, one of the most powerful ever recorded anywhere. It was devastating, and it killed far fewer people than an 8.8 anywhere else would have — because Chile, having recorded the strongest earthquake in human history in 1960, had spent fifty years writing and then enforcing some of the strictest building codes on earth. A country that took its own advice, in writing, for half a century. There is a reason this module's virtue is a synthesis of all the others.

★ LA VIRTUD · LAS OCHO, JUNTAS

Eight modules, eight virtues, and this module had none of its own — because advising somebody well turns out to need all eight at once, and you can watch each one doing its job.

La diligencia (M0) revises the paragraph instead of sending the first draft. La templanza (M1) gives one piece of advice when it has five. La perseverancia (M2) says the useful thing a second time, differently, without getting tired. La cortesía (M3) chooses the register that leaves the other person room to answer. El servicio (M4) spends the advice on them rather than on being right. La responsabilidad (M5) owns what the advice will cost if it turns out wrong. La atención (M6) notices the situation before speaking about it. La prudencia (M7) decides which of the four registers this particular moment is asking for.

None of those is a grammar point. But every one of them shows up in the grammar — in whether you attach a reason, in whether you choose hazlo or te pido que lo hagas, in whether your last sentence returns the decision or takes it. That is why this course teaches virtue and Spanish in the same lesson rather than in different rooms. Aconsejar bien es mirar antes de hablar, y hablar dejando la decisión donde estaba.
